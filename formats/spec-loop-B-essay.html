On software methodology · the spec-loop essay

You bring the idea. The method builds the rest.

Most spec-driven practice assumes you already know what to build. This one begins a step earlier — and that single move, made honestly, is where the whole discipline turns.

Instantiated in Claude Code · dev-loop-kitrev. June 2026
i the premise

The expensive mistake is a confident answer to the wrong question.

There is a failure that no amount of testing catches, because it happens before the first test is written. You describe a system, the assistant builds it competently, and it is the wrong system — not buggy, not slow, simply shaped around a misunderstanding nobody named out loud.1 This is why the method resists the urge to specify immediately. A specification written over a bad shape is a fast way to make a mistake permanent. spec-loop is built around the belief that this is the mistake worth engineering against, and that it lives upstream of the spec, not inside it.

So the flow starts with a conversation, not a document. From a vague idea and whatever reference material exists, the assistant proposes a shape — entities, endpoints, boundaries — and the human accepts it, bends it, or throws it out. Only once the shape is agreed does anything get written down. From there the sequence is disciplined and unglamorous: specify, build, verify, and advance on evidence rather than on anyone's say-so, stopping short of the merge, where a human still decides.

The order is the argument: idea → discovery → shape → spec → build → verify → evidence → human gate.
ii discovery

The interview that refuses to agree with you.

The front of the loop is deliberately adversarial. Two skills do the work — /discovery for when you have an idea but not a shape, and /adr-refine for when the shape is known and only the edges need drawing — and both are forbidden from writing code.2 Both run with Read, Write, Glob, Grep; discovery adds WebFetch to read manuals and PDFs. They generate clarity, never implementation. Their entire job is to produce clarity: to name the contradiction, press on the fuzzy term, ask after the failure mode you skipped.

The governing rule is a single sentence, and it is easy to get wrong: grill, don't agree. A discovery in which the assistant nodded along at every turn has not succeeded quietly — it has failed. Agreement is comfortable and worthless here, because an agreeable interviewer simply inherits your blind spots and hands them back to you formatted as a plan.

The value is in the questions — the contradictions, the fuzzy terms, the missing failure modes, the constraints you never said aloud — not in validation. Friction is the product.

iii the artifacts

The truth is not in the chat. It is in the files.

As decisions settle, the conversation crystallizes into a package written straight into the repository — a glossary in CONTEXT.md, the shape in DOMAIN-MODEL.md, behavior and acceptance and rollback in SPEC.md, one ADR per durable decision, a Definition of Done in ACCEPTANCE.md, and the residual risk and reading-order in RISKS.md and HANDOFF.md.3 The build reads these files, not the transcript. That distinction is the whole point of writing them down. This is not bureaucracy for its own sake.

Consider what happens when the chat ends. A new session, a /clear, a context compaction — each of them severs the assistant from everything that was merely said. Sub-agents cannot read a conversation they weren't part of; CI cannot build from a memory; a teammate arriving next week has no transcript. The versioned file is the only substrate that survives all of them, which is why the method treats it as the single source of truth and the chat as scratch paper.

Chat is where thinking happens. The repository is where it is remembered.
iv spec, decision, law

Three documents, arranged by how much they bind.

It helps to see the three governing documents as a hierarchy of force. A SPEC describes observable behavior — what must happen, with which inputs, outputs, and errors. An ADR justifies a choice between alternatives — why this shape and not another, and what it costs. And a CONSTITUTION forbids: it records the invariants that no decision may override, however the trade-off falls.

The distinction is practical, not academic. An ADR is worth writing only when a decision is hard to reverse, surprising without context, and the outcome of a real trade-off — all three, or it is noise that buries the decisions that matter.4 The Implementation Plan is what makes an ADR executable: the agent reads it and builds without re-asking. Code links back with // ADR: <slug>, so a later supersede can find everything the decision touched. The constitution operates a level above all of it. An ADR may legitimately choose one datastore over another; an ADR may never choose to store a certificate in plaintext, because the constitution has already vetoed that before the alternatives were even laid out. Since version 1.1.0 it is a real layer — versioned, its invariants mapped to CWE identifiers — and a violation is not a warning but a BLOCKER that caps the readiness score and blocks convergence by construction.

v proportion

The apparatus scales to what the change can break.

Rigor that ignores stakes is just ceremony, and ceremony is the fastest way to get a method abandoned. So every change opens with a question about consequence — what can this break, and what does the break cost? — and the answer sizes everything that follows. A minor UI tweak carries a mini-spec and a relevant test. A payments or billing change carries a formal spec, an ADR, and security and rollback gates. A large legacy migration carries something more specific: characterization of the existing behavior first, and a hard no-regression bar against it.

Profile A does not drag the whole machine behind it. Profile E cannot proceed without a baseline of how the old system actually behaves — which is precisely where golden testing becomes non-negotiable.

vi the loop

An orchestrator that converges instead of chasing zero.

The /dev-loop takes the ADRs and the acceptance criteria, builds against them, and runs a severity-gated review cycle that is engineered to stop.5 Three principles hold it steady: converge, don't chase zero; tests are a guardrail, not the finale; and generating tests is not the same as running them. It moves through plan, then a coverage gate that triggers characterization tests when coverage is thin, then a build of one commit per logical step, then the QA loop proper, then cross-repo integration, a final coverage pass, and a pull request — where it halts, because the merge belongs to a person.

The reason it must converge rather than perfect is subtle. An agent told to drive findings to zero will keep finding things, oscillating between two states, burning budget on diminishing returns. So the loop watches for that oscillation explicitly — a fingerprint that repeats with period two — and treats a clean cycle plus a clean static gate as done enough, which is a harder engineering target than "perfect" precisely because it requires knowing when to stop.

vii evidence

The one place the agent is never trusted.

Here is the crack that swallows most agentic workflows. If the same agent that makes a change also writes the record of whether it passed, it can simply write exit_code: 0 and move on, having run nothing.6 This is the maker-≠-checker rule — Anthropic's evaluator-optimizer, articulated December 2024. The one who makes does not graduate the work. The temptation isn't malice; it's the path of least resistance, and a method that relies on the agent's good intentions here will fail quietly and often.

spec-loop closes it by refusing to read the agent's account of reality. The ledger — qa_ledger.py, pure standard library — parses the actual artifacts: the JaCoCo coverage XML, the Surefire test results, the linters' reports. It does not transcribe a claim; it measures a file. And it holds one rule without exception.

Absent is not "OK." A tool that did not run is never invented as green.

An empty report that exists can credit a fix; an absent report means the gate never ran, and is treated as such. This is the difference between a method that produces evidence and one that produces a story about evidence, and it is the hinge the entire guarantee turns on.

viii gates

Facts may block. Guesses may only advise.

Not every check earns the right to stop the work. The architecture draws a bright line: a gate that reads a fact — a byte comparison, a mutation-test result, a coverage number — may block convergence, while a gate that guesses over prose may only advise.7 Osmani's finding sharpens this: one tool caught 93.4% of what four caught. The value is an uncorrelated second opinion, not a stack of similar linters. The reasoning is behavioral, not philosophical. A natural-language heuristic that blocks will throw false positives; the developer, tired of being wrong-flagged, turns it off; and now there is a dead gate where a live one used to be.

So spec-check advises and the abstraction heuristic was dropped from the score entirely, while the golden diff, the mutation check, the structural diff, and the readiness rebuild all block — and they run in CI, not in the developer's face. A method preserves its own gates by being disciplined about which ones are allowed to be annoying.

ix the golden anchor

The check the agent cannot author.

Nearly everything in this method is written by the agent — the discovery, the ADRs, the spec, the code, the unit tests. Golden tests are the single exception, and that exception is the entire reason they exist. A golden captures what the old code actually does — bugs, quirks, and all — by running it, not by reasoning about it, and freezes that as a human-approved reference. After a migration, the new code's output is diffed against it byte for byte.

If the agent authored the golden, it would encode the same partial understanding that lost the logic in the first place — and the diff would pass against its own mistake. The golden is a check on the agent, not one it grants itself.

This is why the discipline around it is strict to the point of being mechanical. The agent may write the capture harness, but it may never generate or edit an .approved file; a hook forbids the write outright, so that the first failing diff cannot be "fixed" by quietly editing the reference.8 The same reason your tracked markdown is never regenerated from scratch: some files are field truth, and field truth is not the agent's to rewrite. The reference is sacred, the corpus is drawn from real production data because a golden only guards the paths you actually exercise, and the whole thing is pinned to a fixed locale so a diff never lies about a comma that was really a decimal point.

x autonomy

A loop that runs itself has to earn the privilege.

There is an outer loop — a heartbeat that would trigger the whole inner machine on a cadence — and the method is unusually careful about when to build it. Four conditions gate the decision: the task has to repeat, automated verification has to exist, the budget has to absorb the waste of retries and re-reads, and the agent has to hold senior tools like logs and a repro environment.9 "Loop engineering" surfaced in a single week of June 2026 and converged on these same principles. spec-loop reached the shape independently; the only difference is that a person, not a cron, pulls the trigger — which is a decision, not a gap. Fail one, and the loop costs more than a good prompt written by hand.

And an unattended loop is an unattended attack surface, which is why autonomy arrives with an operational tax paid into the constitution itself: a secret-scan and dependency-audit before any pull request, an origin check on every skill installed — of seventeen thousand audited, some five hundred leaked credentials — non-verbose logging on long runs, permissions re-audited on a schedule, and a session rotated before it bloats and hangs. The relevant metric, once you do automate, is not tokens or attempts but cost per accepted change; below a fifty-percent acceptance rate, the loop is handing back the review work it was built to remove.

None of which is an argument against the method — it is the argument for where it draws its line. A domain of fiscal gateways, payments, and critical retail sits squarely on the side the playbook itself marks "do not autonomize," and the human gate at the merge is not a limitation the method apologizes for. It is the decision the method was designed to protect.

xi the edges

A mature method names where it does not apply.

The final mark of seriousness is knowing when to step aside. A spike written to learn something gets no spec, only a note of what was learned; a throwaway prototype gets marked as such, isolated, and given a death date. And a production fire gets fixed first — but even then the minimum evidence of what changed and how to undo it, along with the spec and decision record written after the fact, are due within a day.

An escape valve that is designed keeps the method honest under pressure. One that is improvised destroys it — people cargo-cult the throwaways, or abandon the discipline the moment it becomes inconvenient.

What remains, when you set the details aside, is a small and stubborn idea: that the tool should execute, the method should govern, the evidence should decide, and the human should approve — in that order, without exception, and with the versioned repository as the memory that outlives every session.
